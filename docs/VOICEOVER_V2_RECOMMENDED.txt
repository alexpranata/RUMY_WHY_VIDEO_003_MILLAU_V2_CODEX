How did engineers build an eight-hundred-foot concrete pier in a valley... and put a highway on top?

France's Millau Viaduct reaches eleven hundred twenty-five feet at its highest point.

First, engineers drilled deep foundation shafts into bedrock. Then they built seven hollow concrete piers using self-climbing formwork.

Crews poured concrete, waited for it to gain strength, and raised the formwork roughly thirteen feet for the next section. Again. And again.

But that only solved half the problem. The steel roadway still had to cross the valley.

So instead of lifting it into place, crews assembled the steel deck on land, on both sides.

Sixty-four hydraulic translators pushed those sections forward in small steps. Temporary red towers and stay cables supported the advancing decks above the gaps.

After eighteen launching operations, the two halves met high above the Tarn River in May two thousand four.

Engineers raised the remaining steel pylons and tensioned the cables.

By December, the bridge was complete.

They didn't build the road in the sky. They pushed it there.
